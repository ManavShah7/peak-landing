The idea

The ring closes at 90%. On purpose.

Every other tracker is built on all-or-nothing. Weigh the rice. Hit 100%. Miss one day, break the streak, feel like a failure, delete the app.

Peak puts its belief in the shape you see most. That missing slice isn’t a bug in your week — it’s the part you’re allowed to miss. Show up about ninety percent of the time and you are, genuinely, winning.

One brain

Three apps’ worth of your life. One coach.

Your health lives in silos, so the most useful signal — how one part affects another — is invisible. Peak holds all three at once.

Nutrition

Type it the way you’d say it. Scan a label when you want exactness. The twenty foods you actually eat, one tap away.

Training

Your sessions sync in from Hevy. Peak reads them against your sleep and your food, then tells you something you didn’t know.

Companionship

A small circle who can see you showed up. Tap a friend’s ring to nudge them. No badges, no streak guilt.

Logging

Say it like a person. Not like a database.

Food logging is the highest-value, highest-abandonment habit in fitness — not because people stop caring, but because the interface makes the good behaviour expensive.

“2 eggs, a scoop of whey, and a banana”→ 381 cal · 37P · 32C · 12F. Logged, with the time you ate it.
Insights

It earns the right to give advice.

Generic advice is noise. Every insight in Peak comes from your own behaviour, and every one taps through to the chart that triggered it.

“You’ve been consistent with chest 2× over the last 2 weeks, but glutes keeps getting skipped — no direct sets, not trained once in 2 weeks.”Tap it, and you’re looking at the sets-per-muscle chart that said so.
Your circle

Accountability, without the cringe.

Change is hard alone, and the apps that try to help mostly make it worse — childish badges, or streaks that turn one missed day into quitting.

Peak gives you a few people whose rings you can actually see. Describe a challenge in plain words — "8–10k steps a day for 15 days" — and it quietly lives on both your home screens.

Trust

A good coach asks. It doesn’t guess.

The hardest problem in an AI product isn’t intelligence. It’s restraint. When Peak doesn’t know how much milk went into your oats, it asks — because a clarifying question reads as competence, and a confident wrong number reads as a toy.
