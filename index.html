The ring closes at 90%. On purpose.

Every other tracker is built on all-or-nothing. Weigh the rice, hit a hundred percent, miss one day, break the streak, feel like a failure, delete the app.

Peak puts its belief in the shape you see most often. That missing slice is not a bad week — it is the part you are allowed to miss.

Three apps’ worth of your life. One coach.

Your health sits in silos, so the one signal worth having — how each part moves the others — is the one nothing can see.

Nutrition

Type it the way you would say it. Scan a label when you want to be exact. The twenty foods you actually eat, one tap away.

Training

Sessions sync in from Hevy. Peak reads them against your sleep and your food, then says something you did not already know.

Companionship

A few people who can see you showed up. Tap a friend’s ring to nudge them. No badges, no streak guilt.

Logging

Say it like a person. Not like a database.

Food logging is the highest-value, highest-abandonment habit in fitness. People do not quit because they stop caring. They quit because the interface makes the good behaviour expensive.

“2 eggs, a scoop of whey, and a banana”

381 cal · 37P · 32C · 12F. Logged, with the time you ate it.

Insights

It earns the right to give advice.

Generic advice is noise. Every insight comes from your own behaviour, and every one taps through to the chart that triggered it.

“You have been consistent with chest twice over the last two weeks, but glutes keeps getting skipped — not trained once.”

Tap it and you are looking at the sets-per-muscle chart that said so.

Your circle

Accountability, without the cringe.

Change is hard alone, and the apps that try to help make it worse — childish badges, or streaks that turn one missed day into quitting.

Peak gives you a few people whose rings you can actually see. Describe a challenge in plain words and it lives quietly on both your home screens.

A good coach asks. It doesn’t guess.

The hard problem in an AI product is not intelligence, it is restraint. When Peak does not know how much milk went into the oats, it asks — because a clarifying question reads as competence and a confident wrong number reads as a toy.
